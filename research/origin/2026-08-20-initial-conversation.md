## Assistant

Yes. And your instinct is **very much on target**. In fact, you've stumbled into a real field called **personal digital archiving / digital curation**, and archivists are explicitly thinking about exactly this problem.

The interesting part is that **the infrastructure exists, but there isn't really a great "send us your entire digital life and we'll preserve it for 300 years" institution**. That's the gap.

### The basic idea is absolutely legitimate

What you're describing is essentially a **personal archive**:

- email — including mundane stuff, not just "important" emails
- photographs and scans
- school papers
- journals/writing
- videos
- recordings
- text messages
- websites/blogs
- computer files
- metadata about all of the above
- perhaps even the computers/software used to create them

And here's the thing I think you're getting exactly right:

**The historically valuable part isn't necessarily the exceptional stuff. It's the mundane stuff.**

Imagine an archivist in 2126 studying ordinary American life in the early 21st century. A folder containing:

> `vacation_2007/IMG_0034.jpg`

alongside 47 emails about booking the hotel, a PDF of the itinerary, a text conversation about where everyone wanted to eat, and a crappy cellphone video from the trip could be vastly more interesting than someone's carefully written memoir.

The Society of American Archivists actually says essentially this: personal records don't have to belong to famous people to have historical value, and specifically lists emails, diaries, photographs, audio/video, websites, school/professional papers, etc.

And universities are already accepting exactly these kinds of collections. The University at Buffalo, for example, explicitly describes emails, documents, digital photographs, websites, audio and video as historical records and has workflows for preserving donated digital collections.

### The Library of Congress is basically telling you: "Yes, this is a problem."

The Library of Congress has an entire **Personal Digital Archiving** program.

They have specific guidance for:

- photographs
- email
- audio
- video
- documents
- websites

And they explicitly warn that email programs aren't designed for long-term preservation and recommend exporting the actual messages, retaining metadata, keeping multiple copies, and periodically migrating the storage.

They also make an important point that gets overlooked:

**Digital preservation isn't just "put it on a hard drive."**

Long-term preservation involves maintaining the actual bits **and** eventually dealing with obsolete formats, software, codecs, hardware, metadata, etc.

# And there's a fascinating wrinkle: don't curate it too aggressively

Your instinct might initially be:

> "I'll make a really nice archive of the important things."

I'd argue against that.

**Preserve the mess.**

Archivists actually caution against prematurely throwing things away or reorganizing them because the relationships between records can themselves have historical value.

Imagine historians finding your email archive in 2175.

They don't necessarily want:

> "Neo's 100 Most Important Emails"

They might want:

> **37,842 emails from 2004–2039, exactly as maintained by the creator.**

That's much closer to finding someone's boxes of letters.

If I were you, I wouldn't initially think:

## And there is one other important issue: privacy

This is actually the hardest part.

Your email archive contains **other people's private communications**.

Your kids' photographs.

Financial records.

Medical information.

Passwords accidentally sitting in old emails.

Other people's phone numbers.

Potentially embarrassing stuff.

So the archive needs a **deed of gift / access policy**, where you can say things like:

> The collection may not be opened until 2075.

or

> Photographs may be accessed immediately, email after 50 years.

or

> Materials involving living third parties remain restricted.

Repositories already deal with this sort of thing. The Society of American Archivists specifically discusses deeds of gift, restrictions, appraisal, privacy, and access when donating personal records.

## Assistant

Yes. **That is a much more interesting idea than simply "help people archive their files."** And I think it is genuinely plausible as a public-good open-source project.

I would actually separate the problem into **three layers**, because they're different engineering problems:

1. **The Capsule format** — what exactly is being preserved?
2. **The Preservation system** — how do we make it survive 50–200+ years?
3. **The Release system** — how do we ensure the contents aren't accessible before the agreed date?

And I'd make one strong architectural decision right away:

> **Don't invent a new archival format from scratch. Build a thin standard on top of existing preservation standards.**

BagIt already gives us a very good foundation for packaging arbitrary files with manifests and cryptographic hashes. It's an IETF-published specification and is used extensively in digital preservation.

PREMIS gives us a standardized vocabulary for preservation metadata — objects, events, rights, and agents.

So our project could essentially be:

**BagIt + PREMIS + a new "Personal Time Capsule" specification.**

# Then there's your 50-year lock

This is where I'd be **very careful**.

I don't think we should make the archive itself dependent on a cryptographic time-lock.

Instead:

### Encryption should be layered.

Imagine:

```text
                       CAPSULE
                          │
                    AES-256-GCM
                          │
                     master key
                          │
             ┌────────────┼────────────┐
             │            │            │
          Key A         Key B        Key C
             │            │            │
         Archivist     Trustee      Time-lock
```

You could require **2 of 3**, or **3 of 5**, etc.

This gives us protection against several catastrophic scenarios.

For example:

> "This collection cannot be opened before January 1, 2076 unless 3 of 5 designated trustees authorize release."

That's fundamentally different from simply trusting a website.

---

# And cryptographic time locks actually exist

This isn't science fiction.

There are systems implementing actual time-based cryptographic release. For example, TimeLock.dev uses a distributed network where 12 of 22 nodes need to cooperate to release a key at the designated time.

There's also a much simpler model where an external time-lock service publishes the decryption key at a specified time.

But I'd **never make such a service the sole mechanism**.

Because imagine:

> 2026: "We'll release your key in 2076."

> 2041: company disappears.

Oops.

The encrypted archive still needs to be useful and recoverable.

---

# I think the ideal system is actually "cryptographic + legal"

This is the clever part.

Suppose I create a capsule in 2026.

The capsule says:

```yaml
release:
  date: 2076-08-20

  policy:
    before_release: prohibited
    after_release: public

  authorized_release:
    - University of Pennsylvania
    - Library of Congress
    - designated executor
```

Then there are **physical/legal custodians** of key fragments.

Maybe:

```text
Key fragment 1 → Executor
Key fragment 2 → University archive
Key fragment 3 → Lawyer
Key fragment 4 → Trusted person
Key fragment 5 → Time-lock service
```

And the encryption requires 3-of-5.

Now you've got both:

**cryptographic enforcement**

and

**legal/social enforcement.**

That's considerably stronger than either alone.

# And there's a REALLY important distinction

I'd define two dates:

### Preservation date

> When may archivists possess and preserve this?

versus

### Access date

> When may humans actually read the contents?

Those should not be the same.

You might give a university your encrypted capsule in 2026.

They can:

- verify the hashes
- replicate it
- migrate storage
- maintain metadata
- verify integrity
- preserve it

but **they cannot decrypt the contents until 2076.**

That is a very powerful model.

And it's compatible with how real preservation systems think about the problem. LOCKSS, for example, explicitly separates preservation from access policies and supports "dark" archives where material is preserved but not made available.

# The preservation problem is actually bigger than the storage problem

This is another thing I'd bake into the project.

Don't say:

> "Put this on a hard drive and bury it."

Instead:

```text
                 CAPSULE
                    │
          ┌─────────┼─────────┐
          │         │         │
       Copy A    Copy B    Copy C
          │         │         │
       Archive    Home      Cloud
          │
       periodic
       validation
          │
       migration
```

Every copy has SHA-256 manifests.

Repositories periodically verify:

```text
expected hash
      ↓
actual hash
      ↓
match?
```

If not, repair from another copy.

That's essentially the philosophy behind LOCKSS ("Lots of Copies Keep Stuff Safe"), which continually compares distributed copies and can repair damaged copies from peers.

# But before we write code, I want to establish the philosophy

I'd propose these as our initial principles.

### 1. Ordinary lives have historical value

The project makes **no assumption that the creator is famous, wealthy, important, or historically significant.**

The whole point is the opposite.

### 2. The archive belongs to the creator

The creator decides:

- what goes in
- what doesn't
- who gets it
- when it becomes accessible
- what remains private
- what happens if the intended repository disappears

### 3. No proprietary lock-in

A capsule must remain intelligible without our software.

If the GitHub repository disappears in 2090, the capsule shouldn't.

This strongly argues for building on BagIt rather than inventing our own binary container. BagIt is specifically designed to package arbitrary digital content while leaving the payload accessible independently of BagIt metadata.

### 4. Open source forever

I'd use a permissive license for the software/specification—probably Apache-2.0 or MIT, though we'll want to think carefully about whether the specification itself should use something like CC BY.

### 5. Existing standards wherever possible

Don't reinvent:

- BagIt → packaging
- PREMIS → preservation metadata
- OAIS → archival model
- established cryptography → encryption
- established file formats → content
- established archival practices → custody

PREMIS is specifically intended to provide preservation metadata supporting long-term usability, and its current version is 3.0.

OAIS is now ISO 14721:2025, so we should understand it even if we don't require ordinary users to know what the hell OAIS means.

### 6. Preserve the mess

**Don't require people to curate their lives into a beautiful story.**

A 40,000-email mailbox is potentially valuable.

So is the folder called:

```text
C:\Documents and Settings\Neo\My Documents\shit\
```

The capsule should support both raw preservation and human description.

### 7. Privacy is fundamental

Not an afterthought.

The system should assume that personal archives contain:

- private correspondence
- information about living people
- financial information
- credentials accidentally left in files
- intimate photographs
- medical information
- other people's data

### 8. Preservation and access are different things

This is **huge**.

An archive can be preserved in 2026 while remaining completely inaccessible until 2076.

That lets an institution say:

> "We have your archive. We've verified and replicated it. We cannot open it."

That's a much more realistic model than trying to make a hard drive survive 50 years untouched.

# And I'd change one thing from our earlier discussion

I don't think we should initially promise:

> **"Cryptographically impossible to open for 50 years."**

That's a dangerous promise.

Instead:

> **"Cryptographically protected according to a documented access policy, with optional cryptographic time-lock mechanisms."**

Because there are different threat models.

For example, if someone possesses:

```text
encrypted capsule
+
master key
```

the capsule isn't locked.

If someone possesses:

```text
encrypted capsule
+
3-of-5 key fragments
```

we've got a much stronger system.

And if the key material itself is distributed among independent custodians, we have another layer.

We can investigate actual cryptographic time-lock mechanisms, but **the standard should not depend on a particular time-lock service existing in 2076.**

That's a critical design requirement.

# And there's a potentially killer feature

## Capsule inheritance.

Imagine the creator says:

> "If Penn accepts my archive, give it to Penn."

But Penn doesn't exist in 2080.

Then:

> "If Penn cannot accept it, give it to Princeton."

Then:

> "If neither exists, give it to the Library of Congress."

Then:

> "If none of those are possible, give it to any accredited archival institution willing to accept the collection under these terms."

That's something we can actually formalize.

And it means we're not just preserving **data**.

We're preserving **intent**.

### Major technical ideas
